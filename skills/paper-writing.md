# Skill: Paper Writing

Discipline rules for writing an academic paper, distilled page-by-page from university lecture decks on academic paper writing and the graduate writing-preparation series. Consumed by the Researcher role of the Research Iteration Loop (see `research-iteration-loop/`); usable standalone. Companion skill: [paper-search.md](paper-search.md) (how to find the literature this skill teaches you to cite).

---

## 0. The one doctrine

**Clarity outranks everything** — at three levels: logic clarity → structure clarity → language clarity. Clarity beats grammar perfection, beats wit, beats style flourishes. You are writing **to communicate, not to impress**: state facts as simply as possible, even boldly; no one wants flowers of eloquence in a research article. A paper shows **what** was done, **why**, **how**, and **what was learned** — the keyword is **reproducibility**.

## 1. IMRAD — the default skeleton

The four-question logic (each question maps to a section):

| Question | Section |
|---|---|
| What **question/problem** was studied? | Introduction |
| How was the problem studied? | Methods |
| What were the findings? | Results |
| What do these findings **mean**? | Discussion |

Logic shape: **BROAD → NARROW → BROAD** (broad orientation → the specific study → the findings in a wider context). Variants exist (IRDAM, IMRMRMRD, ILMRAD with a literature-review section; Nature puts methods last) — **the conventions of your field and the requirements of your target journal take precedence**. Conclusions are stated **three times**: in the abstract, again in the introduction, again (in detail) in the discussion — this redundancy is by design.

## 2. Workflow: think → prepare → write → revise

1. **Think before you write.** A writing mindset means **logical structuring** of the paper — never write anything before you have critically thought about it.
2. **Prepare.** Write from imitation: obtain highly regarded papers in your area, especially from the journal you plan to submit to, and notice how they are written. Get the journal's template and follow its instructions to authors — manuscripts that ignore them are rejected without review.
3. **Write section by section**, without a predefined order, and don't let yourself be disrupted until a section is done. **Create on the paper, not in your head** — always draft first, revise after. Think in the language you write in: never write one language first and translate into another. **Avoid exhausted writing** — good writing needs a working brain; take breaks.
4. **Revise ~10 times**, then stop ("No manuscript is perfect"). Revision checklist — all 11:
   1. Does the manuscript include all the information it should?
   2. Should any content be deleted?
   3. Is all the information accurate?
   4. Is all the reasoning sound?
   5. Is the content consistent throughout (symbols included)?
   6. Is everything logically organized?
   7. Is everything clearly worded?
   8. Is everything concise (brief, simple, direct)?
   9. Are grammar, spelling, punctuation and word use correct?
   10. Are all figures and tables well designed?
   11. Does the manuscript comply with the instructions?

   Practical writing order from the data outward: **figures/tables → methods → results → conclusion & introduction → title & abstract** — the paper is built around the data, and the "showcase" parts are written last. Before submission, run the four-pass pass: (1) overall layout against the venue's requirements; (2) sharpen the innovation/highlight passages; (3) verify every claim paragraph by paragraph; (4) language polish (get a native or near-native colleague for English).

## 3. Title

- A good title = **the fewest possible words that adequately describe the contents**. It is a **label, not a sentence** — word order carries the meaning, so syntax errors are the most damaging defect ("immediate rejection" territory).
- **Never sacrifice clarity to be witty.** No abbreviations, chemical formulas, proprietary names, or jargon in titles.
- Ban **waste words** at the start: "Studies on…", "Investigations on…", "Observations on…", opening "A/An/The".
- Too-short titles ("Studies on Brucella") exaggerate and get rejected; use specific terms: *with what method, did what thing*. Suggested formula: `*** Method/Design/Approach for *** based on/through ***`.
- Watch faulty word order: "Suppression of Pneumonia **in Mice Induced by** Virus" (reads wrong) vs "**Induced in Mice by** Virus" (right).
- No series titles ("…III"), no hanging titles, no main title–subtitle unless the venue allows.

## 4. Authors and addresses

- An author is one who takes **intellectual responsibility** for the results. Decide the author list **before the research starts**, not at the end.
- Order by **decreasing contribution**; the PI traditionally takes the last (terminal) spot; first author = the leading role, regardless of rank. Don't agree to be listed unless you contributed; don't run a laundry list (keep it ≤5 where honest).
- Consistent name form across papers (given name + middle initial + surname; no initials-only); no degrees/titles after names; mark the **corresponding author**; addresses in the same order as authors, each with email; use ORCID where required.

## 5. Abstract

- The abstract is a **miniature version of the paper** — informative, self-contained, **≤250 words** (or the venue's limit), almost entirely **past tense**.
- Four obligatory elements: (1) principal objectives and scope; (2) methods employed; (3) results summarized; (4) principal conclusions.
- **Never** cite literature in the abstract; never include or refer to tables/figures; never state anything not in the paper; omit obscure abbreviations.
- Write it **neutral** — never witty ("Probably not." is a famous joke, not a model). Every word counts: if 100 words tell the story, never use 200. **Write the paper before the abstract.**

## 6. Introduction — the make-or-break section

The introduction decides whether the manuscript reaches a second-round review. Five-part logical structure:

1. Present the **nature and scope of the problem**, with clarity, and **why the subject area is important** — the hook answers two whys: why this subject, why important.
2. **Briefly review the pertinent literature** to orient the reader, and identify the **gap** this work addresses.
3. State the **objective** (or hypothesis/research question; "in order to determine…" signals it).
4. State the **method** of the investigation (and briefly why that method).
5. Where the venue expects it, end with the **principal results** and conclusions.

Rules: **do not keep the reader in suspense** — no surprise endings, no "see the following for details"; never hold back the important findings until late; a bit of redundancy with the abstract is desirable; define specialized terms and abbreviations here; mention your own preliminary notes/abstracts and closely related companion papers; remember readers may be outside your narrow specialty.

## 7. Materials and Methods

- The only rule: **enough information for a competent colleague to reproduce the work**. Give exact technical specifications, quantities, preparation; use generic/chemical names (trade name in parentheses if essential).
- Present in **present tense**, largely **chronological order**, but group related methods together; use subheadings that **match the Results subheadings**.
- Be precise (temperature, not "heated"); emphasize the **data, not the statistics** — ordinary statistical methods need no citation, unusual ones do. Well-known methods: cite only; new methods: full detail. Passive voice is fine here ("Mice were injected with…"). Use short sentences.

## 8. Results

- Two ingredients: the **big picture** (experiment design philosophy) and **the data**. **Past tense.** Typically the shortest section (~20–30% of the paper).
- Present **representative** data, not everything: "The fool collects facts; the wise man selects them."
- Triage: one or a few determinations → **text**; repetitive determinations → **table or graph** (see §10).
- Never repeat what a table/figure already shows. Bad: "It is clearly shown in Table 1 that…" Good: "Nocillin inhibited the growth of *N. gonorrhoeae* (Table 1)."

## 9. Discussion

- Purpose: **show relationships among observed facts** — interpret, don't recapitulate. Many papers are rejected on faulty discussion.
- Components: (1) theoretical implications and possible applications; (2) conclusions stated as clearly as possible; (3) the **evidence for each conclusion**; (4) **strengths and limitations**; (5) **point out exceptions — never cover them up**.
- End with a short **significance** statement (the "so what"), and avoid cosmic conclusions — "shine a spotlight on one area of the truth." Prefer impersonal phrasing: "It was found that…", "It is concluded that…".
- A standalone **Conclusions** section (when the venue has one) is a higher-level analysis, not a repeat of Results: state what the work means, echo the introduction's promises, keep every sentence grounded in facts shown earlier. Readers decide from title → abstract → conclusions whether to read the body — make each worth it.

## 10. Tables and graphs

**Triage rule** (applies before §8): a few numbers → text (one sentence); repetitive data with no trend → table; repetitive data with pronounced trends → graph; data unworthy of a table are unworthy of a graph ("graphs are pictorial tables"). A photograph only if it tells the story — the best papers can be understood from their figures alone.

Table checklist: only repetitive data, no columns of fixed conditions, no rows that convey nothing; avoid exponents in headings (footnote the convention if unavoidable); concise single-clause title; define abbreviations in Table 1, later tables say "Abbreviations as in Table 1"; self-contained wording (meaningful group names, not "Group 1/2"); analogous tables share analogous formats; mention every table in the text where readers first need it.

Graph checklist: label sizes chosen for final published scale; one message per figure; group related graphs into one multi-panel illustration; consistent symbols across the paper's figure set; don't extend the y-axis beyond what the data demand; standard symbols in order ○ △ □ ● ▲ ■ — if you need more, split the figure; fair axis scales (no exaggeration); with rare exceptions start every scale at zero; understandable without the text.

## 11. Acknowledgements and references

Acknowledgements: first technical help and special materials, then financial assistance; get permission before naming people; be specific about ideas you're thanking for; never write "I wish to thank…" (it means you wish you could).

References:
- Cite when: reviewing literature, your method uses others' work, you compare, you point out significance. List only **significant published** references — no unpublished data, theses, or secondary materials for important claims ("In Press" for accepted ones). Every reference accurate and **cited in the text**; place each citation **at the point in the sentence it applies to** — no handwaving ("Smith's elegant contribution") and no nasty referring ("Smith did not study…").
- Three style systems: **Name–Year** (easy editing; social science), **Alphabet–Number** (saves space), **Citation Order** (numbered by first appearance; IEEE; fastest for readers). Keep the list in complete form; let reference managers handle venue style — but "software is the last resort; it is your critical thinking that matters."
- Favor references from the **past 3–5 years**, plus a few genuine classics. Journal-title abbreviations: "Journal"→"J."; -ology words truncate at the "l" (Physiology→Physiol.); one-word titles are not abbreviated; see ISO 4 / the venue's list.

## 12. Choose the journal before writing

Decide where to submit **before** writing, so the paper is geared to that audience: "the journals publishing the papers you will cite are often journals to consider." A poor choice costs three ways: returned as out of scope; unfair review from unfamiliar reviewers; published where your audience never looks. Avoid predatory journals (no real peer review; aggressive email solicitation; a missing "instructions to authors" page is a clue). Read the instructions fully — if there is a checklist, use it.

## 13. Review papers (when the deliverable is a review)

A review is original research done in the library: critical evaluation + important conclusions + future trends. No IMRAD — expand the introduction, delete methods/results, expand the discussion. Tell a **story** (beginning–middle–end); include a **methods-of-the-review** section (how you gathered, sorted, analyzed — a reader could repeat the review); perform a **critical analysis** (strengths, deficiencies, gaps, methodology variations, conflicting findings); **synthesize, don't summarize** (direction for future research, classification, a new way of thinking, quantitative meta-summary, or new theory); stay focused. Write **less technically** than a research paper — the audience spans related fields and students.
